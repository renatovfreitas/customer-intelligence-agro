Business Insights

This document records the most relevant business insights discovered
during the project.

Insights are continuously updated as new analyses are completed.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Insight 001 — Customer Consolidation

Finding

Several customers were registered multiple times in the operational
system.

Business Impact

Using the customer registration ID fragmented customer purchasing
history and produced inaccurate customer-level metrics and rankings.

Action

Customers were consolidated using standardized Customer Name and
CPF/CNPJ rather than the ERP customer registration ID.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Insight 002 — Revenue and Profit Ranking

Finding

After customer consolidation, the highest revenue customer was also
identified as the highest profit customer.

Business Impact

The initial analysis produced different conclusions because duplicate
customer registrations split customer purchasing histories.

This demonstrated that customer-level profitability analysis depends
directly on reliable customer identification.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Insight 003 — Purchase Frequency and Customer Value

Finding

Purchase frequency is strongly associated with customer economic value.

During the analyzed period:

-   627 customers (approximately 32% of the customer base) recorded only
    one purchase.
-   These customers generated approximately 7% of total revenue.
-   Customers with four or more purchases generated approximately 85% of
    total revenue.
-   Customers with more than 21 purchases generated nearly 50% of total
    revenue.

However, some highly frequent customers generate considerably less
profit than other customers with similar purchase frequency.

Business Impact

Purchase frequency is an important indicator of customer value, but
frequency alone does not provide a complete picture of customer
profitability.

Because transaction history before August 2024 is unavailable due to ERP
migration, customers with one recorded purchase cannot necessarily be
classified as first-time customers.

Recommendation

Frequency should be evaluated together with:

-   Revenue
-   Profit
-   Profit margin
-   Recency
-   Product mix

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Insight 004 — Product Classification

Finding

The original ERP product classification was too detailed for strategic
analysis and contained duplicated or inconsistent product subgroup
registrations.

Business Impact

Commercial analyses became difficult to interpret, and counting
technical subgroup IDs could overstate the number of distinct business
categories purchased by a customer.

Action

Original product subgroups were reorganized into broader Product Groups.

Business-level category metrics use normalized subgroup names where
appropriate rather than relying exclusively on ERP technical IDs.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Insight 005 — Data Quality

Finding

Customer and product registration quality has a direct impact on
analytical results.

Examples identified during the project include:

-   Duplicate customer registrations
-   Duplicate product subgroup registrations
-   Products without an analytical Product Group classification

Business Impact

Data quality problems may produce inaccurate:

-   Revenue rankings
-   Profit rankings
-   Customer segmentation
-   Product diversity metrics
-   Inactivity analysis
-   Product-level analysis

Recommendation

Customer and product registration quality should be continuously
monitored.

Unclassified products should also be monitored as a data quality KPI as
new products are added to the ERP.

Insight 006 — Revenue Concentration

Finding

Customer revenue is highly concentrated within a relatively small
portion of the customer base.

-   The top 20 customers account for approximately 21% of total revenue.
-   Approximately 80% of total revenue is generated by 334 customers,
    representing about 17% of the 1,953 analyzed customers.
-   Customers generating R$10,000 or more individually account for
    nearly 70% of total revenue.

Business Impact

A relatively small group of customers has a disproportionate impact on
company revenue.

However, the top 20 customers account for only 21% of revenue,
indicating that revenue concentration extends across a broader group of
valuable customers rather than depending exclusively on a few major
accounts.

Recommendation

Customer retention and relationship strategies should prioritize
high-value customers while also identifying customers with the potential
to move into higher-value segments.

Insight 007 — Revenue Does Not Fully Represent Customer Value

Finding

Revenue and profit are strongly related, but high revenue does not
always translate proportionally into high profit.

The top 334 customers by revenue account for approximately 80% of total
revenue but generate 75.15% of total profit.

Conversely, the top 391 customers by profit generate 80% of total profit
and account for 82.47% of total revenue.

Among the 202 customers generating at least R$10,000 in revenue:

-   120 (59.4%) have profit margins of 25% or higher.
-   Approximately 79% have margins of at least 20%.
-   Only 14 customers have margins below 15%.
-   Only two customers have margins below 10%.

Business Impact

Revenue provides a strong indication of customer economic value, but
differences in profitability remain relevant when prioritizing
customers.

Most high-revenue customers also demonstrate healthy profitability,
suggesting that low-margin high-revenue customers are the exception
rather than the dominant pattern.

Recommendation

Revenue should be used as the Monetary component of the RFM model.

Profit and Profit Margin should remain complementary customer-value
measures and should be used to refine commercial prioritization.

Insight 008 — Recency and Revenue Concentration

Finding

Recent customers account for a disproportionate share of historical
revenue.

Customers purchasing within the last 90 days represent approximately 30%
of the customer base but account for more than 61% of total revenue.

Customers purchasing within the last 30 days alone represent
approximately 14.4% of customers and 40.4% of revenue.

In contrast, 573 customers (29.3% of the customer base) have not
purchased for more than 365 days and account for approximately 10.1% of
historical revenue.

Business Impact

Recency is strongly associated with customer economic activity and
represents an important dimension for customer segmentation.

However, high recency should not automatically be interpreted as
customer churn because expected purchasing cycles differ substantially
between product categories.

Recommendation

Recency should be incorporated into customer segmentation but
interpreted together with historical frequency, customer value and
product mix.

Insight 009 — Reactivation Opportunities

Finding

Not all customers with high recency have historically low engagement.

Among customers with more than 365 days since their last recorded
purchase, 79 customers had previously completed at least four purchases
during the analyzed period.

This group generated approximately R$198,000 in historical revenue and
R$79,000 in profit, with an average of approximately 7.9 purchases per
customer.

Business Impact

Treating all inactive customers equally would overlook meaningful
differences in their previous relationship with the company.

Previously frequent customers who have stopped purchasing may represent
stronger reactivation opportunities than customers with only one
historical transaction.

Recommendation

Future reactivation strategies should prioritize customers based on a
combination of:

-   Recency
-   Historical frequency
-   Revenue
-   Profit
-   Product mix

A dedicated “Customers to Reactivate” segment or dashboard view should
be considered.

Insight 010 — Low Frequency Does Not Necessarily Mean Low Customer Value

Finding

Some low-frequency customers generated substantial revenue.

Thirty-six customers with three or fewer purchases generated
approximately R$573,000 in revenue.

Most of the high-value, low-frequency customers purchased implements.
Among the high-value customers with only one recorded purchase, the
large majority purchased implements.

Business Impact

Low purchase frequency may reflect the naturally longer replacement
cycle of certain products rather than weak customer engagement.

A customer purchasing an implement may reasonably remain without another
similar purchase for a long period, while the same behavior from a
customer historically purchasing parts or lubricants may indicate a more
meaningful change in purchasing behavior.

Recommendation

Recency and frequency should not be interpreted independently from
product behavior.

Low-frequency customers purchasing recurring categories such as parts
and lubricants may represent stronger reactivation opportunities than
customers whose historical value comes primarily from implements.

Insight 011 — Average Ticket Varies Significantly by Customer Behavior

Finding

The overall average ticket is approximately R$590, but this value hides
substantial differences across customers.

Customers with very high average tickets tend to purchase less
frequently, while customers with moderate tickets often demonstrate
considerably higher purchase frequency.

For example:

-   Customers with average tickets of R$10,000 or more averaged
    approximately 1.5 purchases.
-   Customers with average tickets between R$500 and R$999 averaged
    approximately 14.3 purchases.

Business Impact

Average ticket alone is not a sufficient indicator of customer quality
or engagement.

The company serves different purchasing profiles, including:

-   High-ticket, low-frequency customers
-   Moderate-ticket, high-frequency customers
-   Low-ticket, recurring customers

Recommendation

Average Ticket should be used as a descriptive customer-profile metric
rather than as a primary segmentation variable in isolation.

Insight 012 — Product Groups Have Different Economic Behaviors

Finding

Product Groups exhibit substantially different combinations of ticket
size, purchase frequency and profitability.

Two important examples are:

Parts

-   Approximately R$3.59 million in revenue
-   Average group ticket of approximately R$218
-   Profit margin of approximately 44.9%
-   High transaction frequency

Implements

-   Approximately R$2.01 million in revenue
-   Average group ticket of approximately R$14,183
-   Profit margin of approximately 18.7%
-   Low transaction frequency

Business Impact

The company effectively serves different purchasing cycles within the
same customer base.

Parts tend to generate recurring, lower-ticket and higher-margin
transactions, while implements generate substantially larger but less
frequent transactions with lower percentage margins.

This explains why customer Recency, Frequency and Average Ticket cannot
be interpreted without considering what the customer purchases.

Recommendation

Product behavior should complement the future RFM segmentation.

Customer profiles should identify the customer’s predominant or relevant
Product Groups so that similar RFM scores can be interpreted differently
depending on purchasing behavior.

Insight 013 — Observed After-Sales Parts Purchasing Among Implement Customers

Finding

The initial post-sale cross-selling hypothesis was tested using purchase
sequence rather than simple product co-occurrence.

During the 12-month analytical window:

-   50 customers purchased at least one product classified as
    IMPLEMENTOS.
-   25 of these customers (50.0%) recorded at least one subsequent PEÇAS
    purchase after their first observed implement purchase.
-   Subsequent parts purchases generated approximately R$51,600.85 in
    revenue during the available observation period.

An earlier product-mix analysis showed that 35 of the 47 customers whose
dominant Product Group was IMPLEMENTOS also purchased PEÇAS during the
same 12-month window (74.47%). However, that measure did not establish
purchase order. The temporal analysis provides stronger evidence by
considering only parts purchases occurring after the first observed
implement purchase.

Business Impact

Implement sales can be followed by recurring parts revenue and may
represent the beginning of a broader after-sales relationship rather
than an isolated transaction.

At the same time, 25 of the 50 observed implement buyers did not record
a subsequent parts purchase within the available period. These customers
may represent potential after-sales opportunities, but they should not
automatically be classified as missed opportunities because the
available follow-up period differs according to the date of the
implement purchase.

Recommendation

After-sales monitoring should distinguish between:

-   Implement buyers who subsequently purchase parts.
-   Implement buyers with no subsequent parts activity.
-   The amount of time elapsed since the implement purchase.

Follow-up time was subsequently evaluated. Observed parts penetration
was 18.18% with less than 90 days of observation, 46.67% with 90–179 days
and 66.67% with 180+ days.

Commercial Segments v1 therefore uses 180 days as an operational maturity
threshold: implement buyers with at least 180 days since their first
observed implement purchase and no subsequent parts purchase are classified
as AFTER-SALES OPPORTUNITY. This is a versioned analytical rule supported
by the observed pattern, not a proven causal conversion point.

Future analysis should evaluate comparable 90-, 180- and 365-day cohorts
and extend after-sales analysis to lubricants and other recurring categories.

Insight 014 — Customer Product Behavior Is Highly Concentrated

Finding

Product behavior among the 1,380 identified customers active during the
12-month RFM window is highly concentrated.

-   985 customers (71.38%) generated at least 80% of their revenue from
    their dominant Product Group.
-   A further 239 customers (17.32%) generated between 60% and 80% of
    their revenue from their dominant Product Group.
-   Combined, 88.70% of active customers generated at least 60% of their
    revenue from a single dominant Product Group.
-   PEÇAS was the dominant Product Group for 1,104 customers,
    representing 80.0% of the active identified customer base.
-   IMPLEMENTOS was dominant for 47 customers, while TRATORES was
    dominant for one customer.

Business Impact

Most active customers have a clearly identifiable purchasing orientation
rather than a highly diversified product mix.

This provides important context for customer segmentation because
customers with similar RFM scores may have fundamentally different
commercial relationships with the company depending on their dominant
Product Group.

The high concentration in PEÇAS also highlights the importance of
recurring parts activity in the customer base, while the relatively
small number of implement- and tractor-dominant customers can still
represent substantial economic value because of their much higher
transaction values.

Recommendation

Dominant Product Group and its share of customer revenue should be
incorporated into the commercial customer profile.

Product concentration should be used as contextual information rather
than as an isolated measure of customer quality.

Insight 015 — Similar RFM Profiles Can Represent Different Commercial Behaviors

Finding

The interaction between RFM scores and Product Behavior confirms that
customer inactivity cannot be interpreted uniformly across Product
Groups.

Among high-value customers with intermediate recency (R2/R3 and M4/M5):

-   23 IMPLEMENTOS-dominant customers generated approximately R$425.3k
    in 12-month revenue and R$84.4k in profit, with average frequency of
    2.83 purchases and average recency of approximately 181 days.
-   35 PEÇAS-dominant customers generated approximately R$255.1k in
    12-month revenue and R$97.7k in profit, with average frequency of
    15.34 purchases and average recency of approximately 165 days.

Despite relatively similar average recency, the two groups show
substantially different purchasing frequency and profitability patterns.

Business Impact

A customer with high Monetary value, low Frequency and increasing
Recency may represent normal purchasing behavior when the relationship
is driven by IMPLEMENTOS.

The same Recency pattern may carry a different commercial meaning for a
customer whose relationship is dominated by PEÇAS, where purchasing
behavior is typically more recurrent.

Therefore, generic RFM labels such as “At Risk” can produce misleading
conclusions when Product Behavior is ignored.

Recommendation

RFM should remain the behavioral foundation of customer segmentation,
but final commercial segments should incorporate Product Behavior and
profitability.

Commercial interpretation should distinguish at least between:

-   Recurring customers whose activity is cooling down.
-   High-value equipment customers with naturally longer purchase
    cycles.
-   Equipment customers with an observed after-sales relationship.
-   Equipment customers with potential after-sales opportunities.
-   Dormant customers with historically meaningful engagement.
-   Low-value or occasional customers with lower commercial priority.

Insight 016 — Commercial Segments v1 Creates Distinct Actionable Customer Groups

Finding

RFM, profitability, Product Behavior and after-sales context were combined
into Commercial Segments v1. All 1,953 identified customers were assigned
to exactly one mutually exclusive segment.

-   CORE ACTIVE: 307 customers (15.72%), approximately R$1.56 million in
    12-month revenue and R$624.7k in profit.
-   HIGH VALUE EQUIPMENT: 38 customers (1.95%), approximately R$1.14
    million in revenue and R$236.1k in profit.
-   RECURRING COOLING DOWN: 136 customers (6.96%), approximately R$336.1k
    in revenue and R$135.0k in profit.
-   AFTER-SALES OPPORTUNITY: 8 customers (0.41%), approximately R$113.7k
    in revenue and R$22.0k in profit.
-   HIGH VALUE OCCASIONAL: 22 customers (1.13%), approximately R$109.9k
    in revenue and R$40.3k in profit.
-   DORMANT HIGH POTENTIAL: 113 customers (5.79%), with no current
    12-month revenue but a meaningful relationship in the available history.
-   LOW ENGAGEMENT: 1,329 customers (68.05%), approximately R$310.9k in
    current 12-month revenue.

CORE ACTIVE, HIGH VALUE EQUIPMENT and RECURRING COOLING DOWN together
represent 481 customers, approximately 24.6% of the portfolio, and about
85% of identified-customer 12-month revenue.

Business Impact

The segmentation separates recurring healthy customers, high-value
equipment relationships, recurring customers whose activity is cooling
down, after-sales opportunities, high-value occasional customers,
historically meaningful dormant customers and the remaining low-engagement
base.

HIGH VALUE OCCASIONAL prevents economically relevant but low-frequency
customers from being absorbed into LOW ENGAGEMENT. LOW ENGAGEMENT is
intentionally descriptive rather than prescriptive.

Recommendation

Use Commercial Segments v1 as the initial customer-level commercial layer
for reporting and actionable lists. Preserve the underlying RFM,
profitability and Product Behavior attributes for explanation and future
refinement.

Insight 017 — Mature Implement Buyers Without Subsequent Parts Activity Form a Small, High-Value Opportunity List

Finding

Among 24 implement buyers with at least 180 days since their first observed
implement purchase, 16 (66.67%) subsequently purchased PEÇAS and eight did
not.

Those eight customers form the AFTER-SALES OPPORTUNITY segment. Together
they generated approximately R$113.7k in 12-month revenue, with average
Monetary value of approximately R$14.2k, average frequency of 1.38 purchases
and average recency of approximately 239 days.

Business Impact

The result produces a small customer list that can be reviewed individually
rather than a broad generic cross-selling audience. It also reinforces that
equipment customers can be economically important despite low transaction
frequency.

Recommendation

Use these customers as a commercially reviewable after-sales list while
avoiding the assumption that absence of a subsequent parts purchase proves
a lost sale. Product need, competitor purchases and other external factors
are not observed in the dataset.

Lessons Learned

This project demonstrated that business understanding and data quality
are fundamental before creating dashboards or customer segmentation
models.

The exploratory analysis showed that customer value cannot be
represented by a single metric.

Revenue, profit, purchase frequency, recency and average ticket provide
different perspectives on customer behavior, while Product Group
provides essential business context for interpreting those metrics.

The analysis also demonstrated that generic segmentation rules may
produce misleading conclusions when applied without understanding the
company’s commercial model. In particular, low frequency or high recency
does not necessarily indicate customer disengagement when customers
purchase products with naturally long replacement cycles.

The Product Behavior analysis strengthened this conclusion. Most active
customers have a highly concentrated purchasing profile, and PEÇAS
dominates the relationship for most active customers, while IMPLEMENTOS
and TRATORES represent much smaller customer populations with
substantially different purchase cycles and transaction values.

The after-sales analysis also showed that product relationships should
be evaluated temporally rather than only through co-occurrence. Half of
the observed implement buyers recorded at least one subsequent parts
purchase, but differences in follow-up time mean that this should be
treated as an observed penetration rate rather than a definitive
conversion rate.

Based on the completed Customer EDA, RFM analysis and Product Behavior
analysis, the segmentation strategy should evolve toward an enriched
approach:

RFM + Profitability + Product Behavior + After-Sales Context →
Commercial Customer Segmentation

Revenue remains appropriate for the Monetary component of RFM, while
Profit, Profit Margin, Product Mix and purchase-cycle context provide
additional information for commercial prioritization.

Commercial Segments v1 has now translated these validated behavioral
patterns into seven mutually exclusive customer groups. The next project
phase is Historical Data Assessment and Integration using legacy ERP data
dating back to 2018, with customer-level integration dependent on reliable
entity resolution.

## Historical ERP Integration Findings

### Strong Customer Continuity Across ERP Migration

Of 2,471 distinct document-identifiable customer identities found among
legacy buyers, 2,465 also exist in the current ERP.

This represents a 99.76% cross-system document identity match rate,
indicating very strong customer continuity across the ERP migration.

### Customer Registration Duplication

ERP customer registration IDs cannot reliably represent unique
customers.

Among the 2,465 legacy identities matched to the current ERP:

- 1,273 map to exactly one current registration.
- 1,192 map to multiple current registrations.

Current registration multiplicity among these identities:

- 1 registration: 1,273 identities
- 2 registrations: 1,063
- 3 registrations: 103
- 4 registrations: 19
- 5 registrations: 4
- 6 registrations: 2
- 7 registrations: 1

This supports using a separate analytical customer identity rather than
ERP registration ID as the customer grain.

### Legacy Dataset Coverage

The validated legacy analytical sales layer currently covers:

- Period: 02/01/2018 to 15/07/2024
- Sale-item rows: 228,083
- Distinct sales: 90,815
- Analytical revenue: R$26,813,612.49
- Known profit: R$7,701,172.49

Known profit should not be interpreted as complete historical profit
because some historical items have unavailable or unreliable cost.

Insight 019 — RFM v2 Reveals a Large Inactive Historical Customer Base

Finding

The validated vw_customer_rfm_base_v2 and scoring layer contain:

- 3,109 identified historical customers.
- 1,406 customers with at least one recorded purchase in the 12-month
  window; 1,703 without purchases in that window.
- R$4,194,807.91 in 12-month revenue among identified customers.
- 1,700 customers with RFM scores of 111 (54.7% of historical customers).

The 1,700 RFM-111 customers have recency greater than 365 days. The

All 1,406 active customers have positive monetary_12m (minimum R$2.10,
maximum R$193,736.21); none have zero or negative monetary_12m.

Business Impact

A single 'inactive' label obscures substantial differences in past
engagement and value. The boundary convention must be documented to
avoid apparent count discrepancies.

Recommendation

Keep RFM scores, exact recency, frequency_12m, monetary_12m, and
lifetime_revenue available independently. Avoid equating an RFM-111
score with uniformly low historical customer value.

Insight 024 — Implement Buyers Show Cross-Category Purchasing Gaps

Finding

Among 49 identified customers who bought IMPLEMENTOS in the 12-month
window:

- 37 also bought PEÇAS (75.51%).
- 28 also bought SERVIÇOS (57.14%).
- 27 bought both PEÇAS and SERVIÇOS.
- 10 bought PEÇAS but not SERVIÇOS.
- One bought SERVIÇOS but not PEÇAS.
- 11 bought neither PEÇAS nor SERVIÇOS (22.45%).

The overlap is derived from inclusion-exclusion:
49 - 11 = 38 customers buying at least one of the two categories;
37 + 28 - 38 = 27 buying both.

The 11 without either category generated R$115,832 in implement revenue
and R$124,522 in total 12-month revenue; 10 were classified as At Risk
and one as High-Value Occasional under the descriptive v2 rules.

Business Impact

The 11 customers provide a focused list for assessing possible
cross-selling or after-sales engagement, but missing category purchases
in the same 12-month window do not prove missed sales or establish
purchase order.

Recommendation

Review these accounts individually and consider time since implement
purchase, equipment requirements and existing supplier relationships.
Distinguish co-occurrence from actual subsequent after-sales conversion.
